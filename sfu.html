SFU

Making co-op information clearer, more consistent, and easier to use.

Over eight months with Simon Fraser University’s Co-operative Education team, I worked across UX research, information design, and visual communication to improve student-facing resources and internal materials. Across each project and task, the challenge stayed similar: make complex information easier to understand while working within an established brand and program.

Introduction

Designing across a program, not a single product.

20+ Canvas issues identified
25+ Assets created
4 Teams supported

My work supported different teams, audiences, and formats across SFU Co-op. Some projects focused on how students navigated information, while others involved translating research or program content into clearer communications.

Because much of the material had been created at different times and by different teams, consistency was an ongoing challenge. I needed to understand the existing system first, then determine where design could make information clearer without unnecessarily reinventing it.

Across the term, one question connected much of my work:

How could I make complex or outdated information easier to understand while keeping the program consistent?

01 · UX Audit

20+ issues were making co-op information harder to navigate

One project reviewed SFU Co-op's student-facing Canvas experience. I evaluated the structure and presentation of the content, identifying over 20 issues across navigation, hierarchy, clarity, and consistency.

The goal was not simply to make the pages look newer. It was to understand how students moved through the information, what was being emphasized, and where the structure made resources harder to understand or locate.

Navigation

Could students understand where to go and how different resources were organized?

Information hierarchy

Was the most important information visually prioritized?

Content clarity

Could students quickly understand what information or action mattered?

Consistency

Did different pages feel like parts of the same co-op experience?

02 · Research + Content

Turning hiring perspectives into guidance students could actually use

I also helped create educational content for students by speaking with recruiters, alumni, and people familiar with the co-op and hiring process. These conversations explored what employers looked for, how interviews unfolded, and what students could do to prepare.

My job was not simply to present everything from the interviews. I needed to identify what would be useful to students, organize it into a clear narrative, and translate it into approachable educational content.

Interview / raw information Identify useful themes Structure the story Create student-facing content

Designing for usefulness

Students did not need every detail from an interview. They needed the parts that could help them make better decisions.

The design challenge became deciding what to keep, what to simplify, and how to structure the content so that students could quickly understand the main takeaway.

Research synthesis Content strategy Interview-based research Visual storytelling

03 · Information Design

Making dense program information easier to understand

A recurring part of my work involved turning information, research, and program details into formats students and staff could understand quickly. This included infographics, presentations, PDFs, data visualizations, and other visual materials.

Before deciding how something should look, I focused on the hierarchy of the information.

02

03

The visual design followed those decisions, helping dense information feel more structured and easier to scan.

04 · Visual Communication

Learning the brand quickly meant I could focus on the information

I worked within SFU's visual identity across presentations, emails, PDFs, and student-facing communication. Learning the brand system quickly helped me focus less on reinventing the look of each piece and more on what the audience needed to understand.

Stay recognizable

Adapt to the format

Prioritize the message

Brand consistency supported the information rather than overpowering it.

05 · Design Systems

One-off materials became templates other teams could continue using

Some projects became reusable layouts and templates, helping teams maintain consistency without needing a designer to rebuild every future communication from scratch.

Across the internship, I created more than 25 assets across four teams, including presentations, PDFs, student communications, email materials, and other digital or print pieces.

One-off asset Reusable structure Team-owned template

Designing beyond the final file

This changed how I thought about handoff. The most successful deliverable was not always the most visually complex one. It was the one that was clear enough for someone else to continue using after I left.

Reflection

I learned to design for the system, not only the deliverable

SFU Co-op was one of my first opportunities to design continuously inside an established organization rather than a single class project or product.

Working across teams taught me how quickly understanding an existing design system makes collaboration easier. Following a brand system did not mean using the same layout everywhere. Each project still required understanding the audience, the information, and the context.

The biggest lesson was about handoff. Valuable design work was not necessarily the most visually complex. It was clear, reusable, and structured enough for another person to continue using after I left.
